# Parents and the children's flu vaccine
Subtitle: What helps and what stops parents of preschool children getting their child vaccinated against flu
Round: Round 1
Date: Interviews June 2020 · report draft October 2026
Audience: Product managers, senior responsible owners and clients

## Research questions
- What do parents of preschool children know and believe about flu and the children's flu vaccine?
- How do parents decide, and what helps or stops their child getting vaccinated?
- Where do parents get information, and whom do they trust?

## Executive summary
Status: draft
- Parents in this round were strongly in favour of vaccination. Nobody refused the children's flu vaccine.
- When children missed out, the reasons were practical: vaccine shortages, clinic times during working hours, age cut-offs, and invitations that never arrived [T01] [T03] [T04].
- The invitation letter does most of the work. It prompts action and carries almost all the information parents get about the vaccine [T02] [T10].
- Parents see flu as an ordinary illness for a healthy child. They accept the vaccine because they trust the NHS, not because they fear flu [T06] [T11].
- Few parents talk to a professional about flu, and some who asked questions felt judged for asking [T10] [T12].

## Insight: Children who missed the vaccine were not refused it; their parents couldn't get through
Themes: T01, T03, T04, T22
Quotes: P06-20, P03-19
Status: draft
In this round, every parent whose preschool child wasn't vaccinated either wanted the vaccine or would have accepted it. What stopped them was practical:
- invitations were sent before the vaccine had arrived at the practice
- clinics ran at fixed weekday times that clashed with work or nursery
- parents asked and heard nothing back
- the family had no GP after moving
- the child fell just outside an age cut-off

Parents who kept chasing usually got the vaccine in the end; parents who couldn't chase, didn't.
Implications: Work aimed at persuading parents may miss these families. Bigger gains may come from timing stock with invitations, offering flexible appointments, and following up when a slot is missed.

## Insight: The invitation is the main way in
Themes: T02, T10, T04
Quotes: P04-22, P12-17
Status: draft
Vaccination happened when a letter or text arrived and the next step was easy. Where no invitation came, nothing happened unless the parent was unusually proactive. This happened to a child just outside the age range, a family new to a practice, and a child whose development check never mentioned flu. Because parents rarely talk to professionals about flu, the invitation also carries almost all the information they get.
Implications: How good the invitation is, when it arrives and whom it reaches (including both parents) may matter more than any other single contact. Families who fall outside the invitation system are likely to be missed altogether.

## Insight: Parents vaccinate out of trust, not fear of flu
Themes: T06, T07, T11, T21, T16
Quotes: P09-03, P07-09
Status: draft
Most parents see flu as manageable for a healthy preschool child, and many aren't sure what flu even looks like in a young child. They accept the vaccine because they trust the NHS and see it as partial protection that costs little. For some, that makes the flu vaccine a "bonus" rather than a priority, unlike routine childhood vaccines [T15].
Implications: Because parents act on trust rather than a sense of risk, small practical hurdles can turn a "bonus" into "didn't happen". Making vaccination effortless may do more than warning parents about the risks of flu.

## Insight: Parents look up facts but rarely have a conversation, and questions can feel unwelcome
Themes: T09, T10, T12
Quotes: P11-13, P10-11
Status: draft
Parents go to the NHS website for facts and trust it. Few have a two-way conversation about the vaccine with a GP or nurse. Some who asked questions felt treated as anti-vaccine, so they stopped asking. Parents with nurses in the family or research skills had other people to ask; it isn't clear where other parents would go.
Implications: A non-judgemental place to ask questions could help parents who are in favour of vaccination but curious, and parents who find health information harder to use.

## Insight: Parents see the vaccine as protecting their whole circle
Themes: T13, T14, T23, T17
Quotes: P04-15, P05-09
Status: draft
Parents describe vaccination as protecting grandparents, vulnerable classmates and the household's ability to keep working and caring. Nursery and childcare make exposure feel closer, and family health conditions make it feel more serious.
Implications: Talking about the household and other people matches how parents already describe the benefit.

## Quality and anonymisation
- A script checked every quote in this report word for word against the source transcript, including who said it.
- A stratified sample of the coding was set aside for the researcher to check against the codebook.
- Quotes are verbatim, with transcription errors kept. Names, places, employers, occupations and other identifying details have been replaced with [bracketed] labels. Participants are identified only by number.
- All counts in this report are calculated from the coded data. They count people, not quotes.

## Limitations
Status: draft
- A small, self-selected sample. Several participants worked in health or research roles, so this group may be more health-literate than average.
- The interviews took place in June 2020, during the first COVID-19 lockdown, which shaped how people talked about illness and services.
- Vaccination status was self-reported, and the two groups differ in more ways than vaccination.
- A single interviewer; in one interview the interviewer gave the participant information that may not match programme guidance.
